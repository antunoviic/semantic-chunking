\chapter{Findings and Lessons Learned}
\label{ch:lessons}

\section{Summary of findings}
\label{sec:findings}

The research question asked whether a local language model places better chunk boundaries
than rule-based chunking techniques, and under which conditions. The evaluation of
\cref{ch:evaluation} answers it as follows. The LLM chunker is significantly better than the
splitters that are most widely used in practice and it is at least on par with all other
strategies on documents with technical or poorly preserved structure. Its advantage is
largest exactly where rule-based splitters fail, when the layout of a document is
lost or its paragraphs are longer than the target chunk size.

\paragraph{Better than splitters that cut sentences apart.} The LLM chunker retrieves
significantly better than fixed-size splitting and LangChain's default recursive splitter:
on \code{rfc9110} by $+16.4$~pp at \hitk{1} against fixed-size splitting, and on
\code{nasa} by $+8.2$ and $+7.9$~pp against the two. The \code{nasa} handbook shows the
strength of the approach most clearly: after PDF extraction, its pages form paragraphs that are far longer than the target size, so that the default
recursive splitter falls back to spaces and ends \SI{83.7}{\percent} of its chunks inside
a sentence, whereas the LLM chunker ends only \SI{3.1}{\percent} of its chunks there (\cref{sec:mechanism}). The LLM chunker needs no document-specific separators or
thresholds to achieve this. On \code{wells}, whose short paragraphs the default recursive
splitter keeps whole, no difference is significant.

\paragraph{On par with splitters that keep sentences intact.} Against the recursive
splitter with sentence ends, the tuned semantic chunker and the same chunker without the
model, no significant difference remains. The LLM chunker leads by 0.4 to 4.7~pp on
\code{nasa} and \code{rfc9110} and is behind in five of six comparisons on \code{wells},
by up to 5.8~pp. None of these differences is significant, which indicates that once a
baseline keeps sentences intact, the decisions of the local model bring no measurable
gain.

\paragraph{The optional features do not improve retrieval.} The low-information filter
lowers \hitk{3} by 3.6~pp on \code{nasa} and 1.8~pp on \code{rfc9110} (\cref{sec:filter}).
Heading detection changes no hit rate by more than 0.7~pp, topic labels lower \hitk{1} on
all three documents, and letting the model choose the split point at the size cap makes no
significant difference (\cref{sec:ablations}). Parent-child retrieval raises most hit
rates, but the rule-based splitters gain more from it than the LLM chunker
(\cref{sec:parentchild}).

\paragraph{Cost and conclusion.} LLM chunking needs one model call per decision and is
therefore considerably more expensive than rule-based splitting. For retrieval as measured here, a length-matched recursive splitter
that prefers sentence ends to spaces reaches comparable hit rates, and combined with
parent-child retrieval it reaches a higher \hitk{1} than the LLM chunker on all three
documents. The cost of LLM-based chunking is paid only once, at indexing time, since
afterwards the chunks only have to be retrieved from the vector store. The LLM chunker is nevertheless most useful where the structure of a document is unknown,
inconsistent or lost during extraction, as in PDF files: it keeps sentences intact and reaches the best hit rates of the compared chunking
strategies on \code{nasa} and \code{rfc9110} without any tuning, whereas the rule-based splitters only come close once their separators and chunk size have been adapted to the document. For
well-structured plain text, a length-matched recursive splitter with sentence ends is a cheaper alternative with comparable results.

\section{Lessons learned}
\label{sec:lessons}

The most challenging part of the project was the fair evaluation: building baselines that
are really comparable, measuring significance and separating the contribution of the
language model from that of the sentence splitting. The following lessons come from that
part.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A fair comparison needs more than equal chunk length.} At first, only the
    mean chunk length of the baselines was adjusted to the LLM chunker. Evaluating the first
    results revealed gaps in the experimental design: LangChain's semantic chunker had to
    be tuned before it was a fair opponent, the recursive splitter needed a variant that
    prefers sentence ends, and the same chunker without the model had to be added to
    isolate the contribution of the model.

    \item \textbf{Changes to the plan will happen.} The test configuration changed over
    time. For example, exact search replaced the approximate index, and more baselines and
    a family of 30 tests were added after an external review. Furthermore, the
    low-information filter was removed from the tested arm because it removed passages that
    contained answers.

    \item \textbf{Cached results ensure clean tracking.} Chunking is slow, so the
    chunks have to be cached, and each cache needs a clear fingerprint of its configuration
    and code, so that results from different states are not mixed up.

    \item \textbf{Working with limited hardware.} All runs used a laptop with
    \SI{8}{\giga\byte} of memory, on which chunking a document is slow.
    Computing the embeddings in Ollama instead of the Python process and caching every arm
    separately made the runs possible. The effort of chunking on this hardware is one of
    the reasons why the evaluation covers only three documents.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:future}

The evaluation covers one document per structure class, the chunking model is a local
model with four billion parameters, and the embedding model and the parameter settings
were fixed for the entire project. The most similar LLM-based approach,
LumberChunker~\cite{duarte2024lumberchunker}, was not included as a baseline, so the
evaluation compares the chunker only with rule-based and embedding-based strategies.
Furthermore, the benchmark measures whether a verbatim
passage is retrieved intact, with questions generated by a language model; it does not
measure the quality of generated answers or whether a chunk reads as one coherent unit.
The results on \code{nasa} depend on an extraction that joins each page into one
paragraph, and the ablations were run only with the low-information filter.

Future work can address these limitations with several documents per structure class, a direct comparison with LumberChunker using the same local model and length matching, a
larger chunking model, an end-to-end evaluation of the generated answers, a repetition on
\code{nasa} with a layout-preserving extraction and ablations without the filter. For the
library, returning the offsets of each chunk and the reason for each boundary (topic
decision, size cap or heading) instead of plain strings would make its decisions easier to
inspect in an application.
